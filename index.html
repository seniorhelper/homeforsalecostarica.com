How to Buy — Legally & Safely

The one rule that catches every foreign buyer: the Maritime Zone

Foreigners enjoy nearly identical property rights to citizens — except in a 200-meter coastal strip. Understand this before you fall in love with a beachfront listing.

Verify the title at the Registro Nacional

Request official certifications to confirm whether the property is fully titled (fee-simple) or concession land. Don't assume — beachfront listings are often concession dressed as ownership.

Hire an independent attorney / notary

In Costa Rica only a licensed notary formalizes the transfer. Use one that represents you — not the seller — to run the title search and draft the deed.

Use escrow & sign the purchase agreement

A registered escrow agent holds funds until conditions are met. Closing can even be done remotely via Power of Attorney with someone you trust.

Record the deed & pay closing costs

Budget 4–7% of price: 1.5% transfer tax, ~0.5–0.8% stamps, notary (~1.25% + 13% VAT), and legal fees. The new deed is registered at the Public Registry under your name.

Optional: turn the purchase into residency

A qualifying purchase of $150,000+ can satisfy the Investor Residency (Inversionista) program — renewable temporary residency. Ownership alone doesn't grant status; you still file.

The Complete Guide

Buying a home for sale in Costa Rica: the 2026 authority guide

Search "home for sale Costa Rica" and you'll drown in listings — luxury villas with infinity pools, $199,000 surf-town condos, jungle lots promising ocean views. What almost none of those pages give you is the context that decides whether a purchase is brilliant or a slow-motion mistake: what the property is really worth, what it can earn, what you're legally allowed to own, and what it costs to close. This guide fills that gap with current, verifiable 2026 data so you can shop with the confidence of a local insider.

Why Costa Rica keeps drawing foreign capital

The fundamentals are unusually strong for the region. Costa Rica's economy grew 4.6% in 2025, and in that same year the World Bank reclassified the country as high-income — a rare milestone in Central America. It has been a stable democracy since 1948, famously abolished its army, runs universal healthcare, and posted record foreign direct investment of roughly $5.0 billion in 2024. Tourism, the engine under coastal property values, generated about $5.54 billion in 2025 and now out-earns the combined exports of pineapple, bananas, coffee, and pharmaceuticals. When you buy near a beach here, you're buying into a demand stream backed by record visitor numbers, not a speculative bet.

What homes actually cost in 2026

The national median home price sits near $220,000 — a more honest figure than the average, which luxury beachfront skews upward. Nationally, apartments run about $2,040 per square meter and houses a bit less. In beach towns, starter condos begin around $199,000, larger homes and beachfront villas clear $500,000, and luxury properties pass $1 million. The Central Valley premium suburbs of Escazú and Santa Ana command over $2,500 per square meter for quality condos, reflecting steady corporate and expat demand. Importantly, 2026 favors buyers: closed sales typically land about 7% below asking, with negotiating room of 3–12%, wider on properties that have sat or need work.

The coastal luxury correction is the headline opportunity. Some Guanacaste segments fell 31–36% from their 2024 peaks as the post-pandemic frenzy cooled — bringing trophy properties back within reach of disciplined buyers. Mid-market and urban segments, by contrast, stayed stable, and urban apartments in the Central Valley have even posted double-digit annual growth in select datasets.

Rental yields: the income case

If cash flow is your goal, location is everything. National gross rental yields average about 7.8%, but the spread is wide. Tourist hotspots — Tamarindo, Nosara, Jacó, Manuel Antonio, and Playa Flamingo — routinely produce 7–10%+ gross on short-term rentals, driven by strong, dollar-denominated visitor demand. The catch every honest analyst names: after management, HOA fees, taxes, maintenance, and vacancy, net yields usually settle into the 3–5% range. The exception is well-run, sustainable short-term rentals in the Southern Pacific (Uvita, Dominical, Ojochal) and Osa, where investors report net cap rates of 8–12%. Underwrite on realistic occupancy, not peak-season rates, and the numbers hold up.

The legal layer foreigners must understand

Here's the reassuring part: for the roughly 95% of Costa Rican property that is titled fee-simple, foreigners have the same rights as citizens — to buy, sell, lease, mortgage, and inherit freely, with no nationality-based quotas, even on condos. The one major exception is the Maritime Zone (Zona Marítimo Terrestre) under Law 6043. It runs 200 meters inland from the high-tide line. The first 50 meters are public — nobody can own them, and there are no truly private beaches in Costa Rica. The next 150 meters are concession land: essentially a government lease, typically 20 years and renewable, where foreigners without five years of residency may hold only up to 49% directly. Because only about 5% of the country's beachfront is titled fee-simple, the single most expensive mistake foreign buyers make is assuming a "beachfront" home is fully owned when it's actually concession. Always verify at the Registro Nacional before you sign.

Closing costs, taxes, and financing

Plan for total closing costs of 4–7% of the purchase price, with 5–6% typical. The largest line is the 1.5% transfer tax, calculated on the higher of the declared price or registered fiscal value. Add documentary and registry stamps (about 0.5–0.8%), notary fees (roughly 1.25% plus 13% VAT), legal fees, and an optional escrow fee (~0.25%). Many of these items are negotiable and often shared between buyer and seller, so the allocation belongs in your purchase agreement. Ongoing, property tax is a modest 0.25% of registered value annually, with a separate luxury tax (Impuesto de Lujo, tiered) on higher-value construction. Financing exists but is conservative: USD mortgage rates for foreigners run roughly 7–10%, with 30–50% down depending on residency. Most foreign buyers pay cash or finance abroad.

Where the smart money is looking

Guanacaste and the North Pacific — Tamarindo, Nosara, Playa Flamingo, Playas del Coco — remain the luxury and rental heartland, with Liberia's international airport (LIR) adding routes and the strongest demand. The Nicoya Peninsula's Santa Teresa has become a digital-nomad and surf magnet with rising average sale prices. The Central Pacific (Jacó, Herradura/Los Sueños, Manuel Antonio) trades a 1.5–2 hour San José drive for resilient condo demand and domestic weekend tourism. The South Pacific — Uvita, Dominical, Ojochal — is the appreciation story, with up to 42% year-over-year price growth in 2024–25 and an eco-luxury positioning that's pulling investors away from pricier Guanacaste. And the Central Valley (Escazú, Santa Ana, Atenas, Heredia) is the full-time-living and stability play: best healthcare, international schools, and steady 4–8% appreciation, with infrastructure like the planned GAM electric train and Ruta 27/32 upgrades quietly repricing nearby neighborhoods.

A town-by-town buyer’s snapshot

Averages hide the deals. At the town level, the same ‘Costa Rica’ budget buys radically different lifestyles and returns. In Tamarindo, a turnkey two-bedroom condo near the beach typically trades in the high-$300Ks to mid-$500Ks and rents nightly to a deep, year-round tourist pipeline — the easiest market for hands-off vacation-rental income, though also the most competitive on price. Twenty minutes south, Playa Flamingo and Potrero skew higher-end and quieter, favored by buyers who want a marina-adjacent address without Tamarindo’s foot traffic.

On the Nicoya Peninsula, Nosara and Santa Teresa are the wellness-and-surf magnets — Blue Zone longevity branding, a remote-worker crowd, and land values that have climbed steadily as access roads improve. Expect to pay a premium for ocean-view lots and titled beachfront, which is genuinely scarce here. In the Central Pacific, Jacó and Los Su&entilde;os offer the shortest drive from San José’s main airport, a 200-slip marina, and gated resort condos where a two-bedroom can gross $40,000–$55,000 a year in rental income for owners who market well. The South Pacific — Uvita, Dominical, Ojochal — is the appreciation story, with some submarkets posting double-digit annual volume growth as the ‘Costa Ballena’ corridor matures.

If your goal is stability and everyday living rather than tourist income, the Central Valley — Escazú, Santa Ana, Atenas, Grecia — is where most full-time foreign residents settle: spring-like climate year-round, the best private hospitals in Central America, international schools, and steadier 4–8% appreciation without coastal volatility. And on the Caribbean side, Puerto Viejo and the Limon coast remain the value play — the lowest entry prices in the country for buyers willing to trade a longer logistics chain for jungle-meets-sea character that the Pacific lost years ago.

The honest risk picture

No serious guide sells without caveats. The biggest macro risk is an external demand shock — a U.S. recession would thin discretionary foreign investment, since American buyers make up the bulk of coastal demand (an estimated 40–60% of transactions in towns like Tamarindo, Jacó, and Manuel Antonio). Stricter enforcement of short-term rental rules could compress yields more than expected. Some markets are openly overhyped — locals point to ultra-premium Playa Flamingo trophies and central Jacó's "easy Airbnb" condos — and currency swings in the colón affect costs. The mitigants are equally real: a constructive 3–5 year outlook of 4–8% annual appreciation in well-located areas, limited prime beachfront and urban land, the remote-work wave, and credible 10-year models projecting cumulative growth of roughly 55–85%. The throughline: buy titled property in an established or clearly-improving area, underwrite conservatively, and use independent legal counsel.

Financing a Costa Rica purchase as a foreigner

Most foreign buyers in Costa Rica pay cash, and there's a reason: local mortgage financing is available but conservative and expensive by North American standards. USD-denominated loans from Costa Rican banks typically carry interest rates of 7–10% for well-qualified borrowers, and non-residents usually land at the upper end of that band. Down-payment requirements run 30–50% depending on your residency status, and the underwriting timeline is slower than buyers expect. This is where residency pays a second dividend — holding temporary or permanent residency materially improves your odds of bank approval and can unlock better terms. Many investors instead tap a home-equity line or refinance against property in their home country, then buy in Costa Rica with cash to strengthen their negotiating position. Cash offers close faster and command deeper discounts, especially on listings that have lingered. Whatever route you choose, confirm current down-payment thresholds directly with the lender or with SUGEF, Costa Rica's financial regulator, because requirements shift.

Holding costs & taxes after you own

The closing is one-time; ownership is ongoing. Costa Rica's annual property tax (impuesto sobre bienes inmuebles) is low — about 0.25% of the registered value — collected by the municipality and used for local services. Higher-value homes also pay the luxury tax (Impuesto de Lujo), a tiered annual levy that applies once construction value passes roughly ₡138 million (about $275,000), adding up to a few tenths of a percent. Condo and resort-community owners should budget HOA fees, which range from modest to substantial — master-planned communities often run $500–800+ per month for amenities and management. Rental income carries its own tax: for non-residents under the simplified system, the effective rate is around 12.75% (15% on 85% of gross income). None of these are deal-breakers, but they belong in your net-yield model from day one. The honest net-yield math — gross rent minus management, HOA, taxes, maintenance, insurance, and vacancy — is what separates a property that performs from one that merely looks good in a listing.

A due-diligence checklist that protects your money

The difference between a clean Costa Rica purchase and a horror story is almost always due diligence. Before you wire a cent, work through these:

  • Confirm the title type. Pull official certifications from the Registro Nacional to verify whether the property is fee-simple or concession land — never take the seller's word for beachfront ownership.
  • Run a clean title search. Have your independent attorney check for liens, mortgages, easements, squatters' rights (a real issue on rural land), and boundary disputes.
  • Verify water rights. On lots and rural homes, confirm a legal water source — an AyA or ASADA letter (carta de agua) — because no water means no building permit.
  • Check the zoning plan (Plano Regulador) for any coastal or development-restricted parcel before assuming you can build what you envision.
  • Use registered escrow. Never pay the seller directly; a licensed escrow agent holds funds until every condition is satisfied.
  • Hire your own attorney. The notary who formalizes the deed should represent you, not double as the seller's agent. Independent counsel is the cheapest insurance you'll buy.

Living there: relocation, residency & lifestyle

For many buyers, the home is also the doorway to a life change. Costa Rica's pull is well documented: a stable democracy, universal healthcare (the public Caja system plus affordable private care), and one of Latin America's safest reputations for residents. The Central Valley — San José, Escazú, Santa Ana, Atenas, Heredia — offers the best blend of infrastructure, international schools, healthcare, and a spring-like climate year-round, which is why corporate transferees and retiree families concentrate there. Beach towns suit a different rhythm: Tamarindo and Santa Teresa draw digital nomads and remote workers, while Atenas and Nosara are perennial retiree favorites. Three residency pathways matter most: Inversionista (the $150,000 investment route, satisfiable through your property purchase), Pensionado (for those with at least $1,000/month in qualifying pension income), and Rentista (proving $2,500/month of guaranteed income for two-plus years). After three years of legal residency you can apply for permanent residency, with citizenship possible after seven. The remote-work movement has only accelerated this — recent reforms have eased requirements for location-independent workers, fueling demand for ocean-view, mountain-climate lots.

Thinking about the exit before you enter

Sophisticated investors plan the sale on the day they buy. Costa Rica has no nationwide MLS like the U.S., so liquidity varies by market: established beach towns and Central Valley suburbs transact reasonably often, while remote luxury or single-niche resort product can sit. Two structural details shape your exit. First, many properties are held inside a Costa Rican corporation (sociedad anónima); buying or selling the shares of that company — rather than transferring the property itself — is a common, legal way to streamline a deal, though it must be done with proper counsel and full transparency on value. Second, the government has tightened enforcement on under-reported transfer values, so record the true price and keep everything clean; it protects your eventual resale and your tax position. Capital appreciation does the heavy lifting on returns here: with credible models projecting 55–85% cumulative growth over a decade in well-located areas, the buyers who win are the ones who bought titled property in a durable location, ran honest numbers, and were patient.
